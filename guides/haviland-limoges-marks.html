How to identify Haviland Limoges marks

Three different Haviland firms made porcelain in Limoges. Here is how to tell Haviland & Co., Theodore Haviland and Charles Field Haviland apart, and why most pieces carry two marks.

Three Havilands, not one

The first thing to understand is that "Haviland Limoges" is not a single company. David Haviland, an American importer from New York, settled in Limoges in the 1840s and built Haviland & Co. into the largest porcelain firm in the city. His nephew Charles Field Haviland ran a separate factory under the CFH name from the late 1860s. After David's death, his sons Charles Edward and Theodore fell out, and in the early 1890s Theodore left to found his own works, Theodore Haviland & Co. For roughly fifty years there were two rival Haviland factories operating in Limoges at the same time, until Theodore's firm acquired the original Haviland & Co. in the 1940s.

So when you turn a plate over you may see any of three family names, and each has its own sequence of marks.

Why most pieces have two marks

Haviland porcelain was usually marked twice. The first mark went on under the glaze at the factory that made the blank white piece; collectors call it the whiteware or factory mark. It is typically green and reads something like H & Co with an L for Limoges, or Haviland France. The second mark went on over the glaze when the piece was decorated, often in red, and reads Haviland & Co. Limoges or Decorated by Haviland & Co. If a blank was sold undecorated and painted by someone else, you will see the green factory mark alone, sometimes with a china painter's signature and date.

A green underglaze mark with no red decorator mark does not make a piece a fake. It means the white blank left the factory undecorated. Many of the finest hand painted Haviland pieces were done this way by studio and amateur painters in America between about 1880 and 1920.

Exact date ranges are argued over by reference authors and the same mark could stay in use for decades, so treat the periods above as brackets, not birthdays.

Theodore Haviland marks

Theodore Haviland's marks almost always include his full name, which makes them easy to separate: Theodore Haviland Limoges France in a horseshoe or straight line, sometimes with the words Mont Mery (the site of his factory), and later versions with a stylised TH. From the late 1930s Theodore Haviland also produced in the United States, and pieces marked Theodore Haviland New York are American, not French. After the two firms were combined, marks reading Haviland Limoges France without "& Co." generally belong to the post war company.

Charles Field Haviland: CFH, GDM and GDA

Charles Field Haviland's own factory marked its blanks CFH. When he partnered with the firm of Gerard, Dufraisseix and Morel in the 1880s the mark became CFH / GDM, usually in green underglaze. After Morel left and Abbot joined, around the turn of the century, it became GDA (Gerard, Dufraisseix & Abbot), and GDA pieces continued well into the twentieth century. A red overglaze Ch. Field Haviland Limoges mark indicates factory decoration. So a CFH/GDM mark places a piece broadly in the 1880s to 1890s, and a GDA mark later.

Johann Haviland is not Limoges

You will also see Johann Haviland Bavaria. This was a German factory founded in the early twentieth century by a grandson of David Haviland. It is a perfectly good maker, but it is Bavarian porcelain, not Limoges, and it is priced accordingly.

Dating by pattern and shape

Haviland & Co. produced thousands of patterns and the factory rarely printed pattern names. Collectors rely on the Schleiger numbering system, a reference catalogue of drawings that assigns each pattern a number, so a listing that says "Schleiger 29" is telling you the pattern has been matched to that reference. Blank shapes (the moulded form of the plate or cup, such as Ranson or Marseille) are a second clue and are often named in serious listings.

What to check before you buy

  • Ask for a photograph of the mark. Both marks if there are two.
  • Check that the decoration and the mark agree. A red decorator mark on a piece that is obviously amateur painted is a warning sign.
  • Gilt wear on rims is normal for a hundred year old plate and is usually disclosed. Chips, hairlines and repairs should always be disclosed.
  • Replacement buyers: match the blank shape as well as the pattern, because the same floral spray was used on several shapes.

Every Haviland piece in our decorative plates and teacups collections shows the mark in the listing photographs.

This guide reflects our experience handling pieces in the shop and standard references. Marks vary and were sometimes reused; if you are valuing a piece for insurance or sale, confirm with a specialist appraiser.
