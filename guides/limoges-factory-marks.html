Limoges factory marks: a working list

Limoges is a city, not a company. Dozens of factories used the name. This list covers the marks you will actually meet on hand painted plates, pitchers and teacups, and what each one tells you.

"Limoges" means a place

Limoges is a city in central France that has made hard paste porcelain since the late eighteenth century, after kaolin clay was found nearby. By 1900 there were dozens of factories there, and most of them stamped the word Limoges on their wares. The word on its own therefore tells you where a piece was made and roughly what it is made of (fine, white, translucent hard paste porcelain), but not who made it. For that you need the initials and symbols around the word.

Almost every Limoges piece carries a green underglaze factory mark on the white blank. If it was decorated at the factory it will have a second, overglaze decorator mark, often red. If it was decorated elsewhere, by an American china painter for example, you will usually see only the green mark plus a painted signature and sometimes a date.

Reading a mark step by step

  1. Find the underglaze mark. Run a fingernail over it; a factory mark under the glaze feels smooth and cannot be scratched off. That is the maker of the blank.
  2. Look for a second, overglaze mark. If present, the factory (or an importer's decorating studio) decorated the piece. If absent, look for a painted signature; the piece was decorated outside the factory.
  3. Note the word France. From 1891 the United States required imported goods to be marked with the country of origin, so "France" on a mark generally means 1891 or later. "Limoges" alone, without France, is usually earlier or was not made for the American market.
  4. Check the decoration matches the era. Art Nouveau florals and heavy gilt suit 1895 to 1915; simple bands and transfers suit the 1920s and 1930s.

Two traps

American "Limoges." The Limoges China Company of Sebring, Ohio, used the word Limoges on American made semi porcelain for decades. Its marks usually say "Limoges China" and often "U.S.A." or "Sebring". It is collectable in its own right, but it is not French porcelain and should not be priced as such.

Modern trinket boxes. The small hinged boxes marked "Limoges France" and "Peint Main" are late twentieth century and current production. They are charming, and some are hand painted, but they are not antique.

Signed and dated pieces

Hand painted Limoges blanks decorated in America between roughly 1880 and 1920 are frequently signed on the front and sometimes dated on the back. A signature and date add interest and help with provenance, but the value depends far more on the quality of the painting than on the name, unless the painter was a known studio artist.
