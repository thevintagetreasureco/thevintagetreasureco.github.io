How to tell real Swarovski crystal

The logo etched into the base, the quality of the cut, the box and the certificate: what to check before you buy a Swarovski Silver Crystal figurine, and how to date one from its logo.

Start with the logo

Swarovski has etched a logo into almost every Silver Crystal figurine since the line began in the late 1970s, and the logo changed over time, which makes it the single best dating tool.

LogoPeriodNotes
Block "SC" letters, sometimes with an edelweiss flower abovelate 1970s to about 1988The earliest figurines. The SC stands for Swarovski Crystal. Some very early pieces carry no mark at all.
Swan logoabout 1988 or 1989 to todayA stylised swan, usually etched on the base or an unobtrusive facet. Modern pieces may add a small registered mark or the word Swarovski.

The etching is tiny, often only a few millimetres, and is easiest to find with a loupe or a phone camera on macro, tilting the piece under a lamp until the frosted mark catches the light. It is usually on the underside of the base or on a lower facet.

Absence of a logo is a red flag on anything sold as a 1990s or later figurine. On pre-1988 pieces it is possible, but you should then rely much more heavily on the cut quality and the box.

Look at the crystal itself

Swarovski's reputation rests on precision cutting and exceptional clarity. Genuine pieces have perfectly flat facets that meet in crisp, sharp edges, no bubbles or cloudiness inside the crystal, and a rainbow sparkle that is even across the whole piece. Fakes and lower grade "crystal" typically show rounded or slightly wavy facet edges, a greyish or yellowish cast, tiny bubbles, and visible glue where components meet. Swarovski pieces are assembled with almost invisible bonding; if you can see a milky glue line or a blob of adhesive, be suspicious.

Historically Swarovski crystal was a high lead content glass, which gives it noticeable weight and brilliance. Since 2012 the company has used a lead free formula it calls Advanced Crystal, so weight alone is no longer a test for newer pieces.

Box, certificate and product number

Figurines were sold in a fitted box with the Swarovski logo and, for most of the range, a certificate of authenticity. The box design changed over the years (early figurines came in a blue box; later ones in a grey or white box, then the current designs), so the box style is itself a rough date. A retired figurine with its original box and certificate is generally worth considerably more than the same piece loose, and serious buyers expect the certificate for higher value pieces.

Each figurine has a product number (for example 7611 for the panda, or a longer article number on later pieces). The number appears on the box and certificate and is how collectors' reference guides index the piece. If a seller cannot give you the product number, or the number does not match the piece, walk away.

Retired pieces, designers and the SCS

Swarovski retires designs regularly, and it is the retirement that drives the collector market. The Swarovski Collectors Society (SCS) was founded in 1987 and each year issued a members only annual edition; those pieces are marked as SCS editions and command the highest prices in the Silver Crystal range. Designer names matter too: Max Schreck, Adi Stocker, Anton Hirzinger, Gabriele Stamey, Michael Stamey and Martin Zendron are among the names collectors look for, and the designer is often stated on the certificate.

Condition

Crystal chips. Check every tip, ear, beak and tail under a bright light. Small chips are sometimes polished out and resold as perfect; look for a facet that is smaller or a different shape from its neighbour. Colour components (a frosted branch, a black bead eye, a gold plated stem) should be tight and uniform, and any looseness suggests a repair.
