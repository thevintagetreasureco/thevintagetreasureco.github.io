Caring for antique porcelain

Hand washing, gilt, plate hangers, sunlight and storage. The short list of habits that keep a hundred year old plate from becoming a ninety nine year old shard.

Washing

Never put antique porcelain or bone china in a dishwasher. The heat, the detergent and the vibration strip gilding, dull enamel colours and can open a hairline into a crack. Wash by hand in lukewarm water with a little mild dish soap, using a soft cloth rather than a sponge with a scouring side. Line the sink with a folded towel so a slip does not meet stainless steel. Rinse and dry immediately; do not leave pieces to soak, and never plunge cold porcelain into hot water or the reverse, because thermal shock is the most common cause of cracks in otherwise sound pieces.

Stains inside cups and on crazed glazes can sometimes be lifted with a paste of baking soda and water left for a few minutes, or, for stubborn tea staining, a denture cleaning tablet dissolved in warm water. Avoid bleach and avoid anything abrasive on gilt.

Gilding and enamels

Gold on antique porcelain is a thin layer fired onto the glaze, and on many pieces it is already worn from a century of use. Every wash removes a little more. If a piece has bright, unworn gilt, it is worth keeping it for display rather than daily use.

Displaying plates

Spring loaded wire plate hangers are the single biggest cause of chipped rims we see. The wire hooks bite into the edge and, over years, chip the glaze or crack the plate. Use an adhesive disc hanger rated for the plate's weight (applied to a clean, dry back and allowed to cure fully), or display plates on a stand or a plate rail with a lip. If you must use a wire hanger, choose one with vinyl coated hooks and check that it is not under tension.

Keep plates and figurines away from the edges of shelves and out of the path of doors and curtains. Museum wax or a small ring of putty under a figurine or vase keeps it from walking off a shelf when a truck goes by.

Sunlight and heat

Porcelain itself does not fade, but some overglaze enamels and hand painted decoration can dull over decades of direct sun, and heat from a radiator or fireplace will stress a piece that already has a hairline. Direct sunlight also shows up dust, which is a nuisance more than a danger.

Storing china

Stack plates with a soft separator between each one: felt, coffee filters, or paper towels. Never stack cups inside one another; store them upright or on their sides with padding. Store in a dry cupboard rather than a garage or attic, where temperature swings and damp encourage crazing and mould in old crazing. Quilted china storage cases are inexpensive and work well.

Repairs

A chip or hairline does not always need fixing, and a bad repair lowers value more than the original damage. If a piece matters to you, a professional ceramic restorer can fill and inpaint chips almost invisibly; superglue at the kitchen table cannot, and it yellows. Whatever you do, disclose the repair if you later sell.

Packing for shipping

If you are sending a piece, do what we do: wrap each item individually in bubble wrap with the handle and any projecting parts padded first, place it in a box with at least two inches of cushioning on every side, and put that box inside a second box with more cushioning. Plates ship on edge, not flat. Mark the box fragile, but pack it as though nobody will read the label.

This guide reflects our experience handling pieces in the shop and standard references. Marks vary and were sometimes reused; if you are valuing a piece for insurance or sale, confirm with a specialist appraiser.
