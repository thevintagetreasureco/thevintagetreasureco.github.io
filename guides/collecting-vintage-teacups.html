Collecting vintage teacups and saucers

Bone china versus porcelain, the English makers worth knowing, how to date a backstamp and what condition words actually mean. A starting guide for teacup collectors.

Bone china or porcelain?

Most English teacups are bone china, a formula that includes bone ash and produces a very white, very translucent, surprisingly strong body. Hold the cup up to a window: bone china glows and you may see the shadow of your fingers through it. Continental teacups (Limoges, Bavaria, Austria) are hard paste porcelain, which is harder, slightly cooler or greyer in tone, and often thinner at the rim. Japanese cups can be either, and much mid century Japanese china is a lighter, more fragile porcelain. None of these is better than the others; they are different materials with different feels, and collectors usually settle on one.

English makers you will see again and again

MakerKnown for
AynsleyFounded in the eighteenth century in Stoke on Trent. Rich colour grounds (cobalt, coral, green) with gilt and a floral interior; also the corset shaped cup and pattern names like Pembroke and Orchard Gold.
ParagonHeld royal warrants (the "By Appointment" backstamp), fine bone china with floral sprays and pastel grounds. A single or double warrant mark helps date the piece.
Royal AlbertOld Country Roses, introduced in 1962, is the best selling bone china pattern ever made. Earlier Royal Albert florals from the 1930s to 1950s are more delicate and increasingly collected.
ShelleyThe collector's favourite for shape: Dainty, Oleander and Queen Anne forms, produced between the 1920s and 1966. Prices run higher than most.
Windsor, Elizabethan, Rosina, Colclough, Queen Anne, Royal ValeMid century makers of affordable, pretty bone china. Ideal for mismatched tea services and for starting a collection without spending much.

Reading the backstamp

  • "England" on its own generally indicates 1891 or later, when the United States began requiring country of origin on imports.
  • "Made in England" is generally twentieth century, most commonly from the 1920s onward.
  • "Bone China" in the mark is typically twentieth century.
  • Pattern numbers (a painted number like 2042 or a printed number under the mark) identify the decoration and can be looked up in maker references and replacement databases.
  • Registered design numbers ("Rd No") date the design registration, not the piece, but give an earliest possible date.

Shapes

Teacup shape is a language of its own. A footed cup sits on a small pedestal; a corset cup narrows at the waist; a demitasse is a small coffee cup; a trio is a cup, saucer and matching side plate. Shape names are maker specific, and collectors of Shelley and Aynsley in particular buy by shape as much as by pattern.

What condition words mean

  • Chip: a piece missing, usually at the rim or foot. Always a defect; should be shown in a photograph.
  • Hairline: a fine crack, sometimes only visible against the light or by a dull note when the cup is tapped. Also a defect.
  • Crazing: a network of fine lines in the glaze. Common in older earthenware, unusual in good bone china, and a sign of age or heat stress. Reduces value.
  • Gilt wear: rubbing of the gold on rims and handles from use and washing. Normal and generally acceptable on antique pieces, but it should be disclosed and it affects price.
  • Utensil marks: grey scratches inside a cup or on a saucer from spoons. Minor, but worth knowing about.

A simple test at home: hold the cup loosely by the foot and tap the rim with a fingernail. Sound bone china rings clearly; a hairline gives a flat thud.

Buying for use versus display

If you will actually drink from the cup, avoid crazing and hairlines, which trap liquid and can stain or fail. If you will display it, a small hairline on the underside of a saucer may be an acceptable trade for a rare pattern at a low price, as long as the seller told you about it.
